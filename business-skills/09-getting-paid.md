# Lesson 09 — Getting Paid

*Unlocked by completing Category 09 at 100%. Business Skills ladder, step 9 of 11.*

You can be an excellent tester and still starve if money mechanics are sloppy. Getting paid is not an awkward afterthought; it is a system you design once and run calmly forever. The system has one goal: you should never be in the position of having delivered everything and hoping.

## The golden rule: money before work, in stages

Never finance your client's security with your own unpaid labor. Standard professional structures:

- **Deposit before start.** Commonly 30–50% of the fixed price, paid before the testing window is reserved. A client who will not pay a deposit is telling you how the final invoice will go. Believe them.
- **Balance on delivery.** The remainder invoiced when the report is delivered (Lesson 08), with defined terms (below). Not "when they get around to it."
- **Milestones for larger engagements.** Long or phased work splits into milestone payments tied to deliverables: deposit → end of testing → report delivery → retest completion. Each stage small enough that neither side carries dangerous exposure.

Retest-only and repeat work can run lighter terms for proven clients — trust is earned in both directions, and your terms can relax *after* a client has a payment history, never before.

## The invoice: boring, complete, immediate

An invoice that gets paid fast contains, every time:

- Your legal name/business name, address, and tax ID where applicable
- Client's legal name and billing contact (ask for this in the proposal stage — chasing it after delivery delays everything)
- Invoice number (sequential, never reused) and issue date
- What it is for, in the client's language: "Web application penetration test — [product] — [dates] — per proposal dated [date]"
- Amount, currency, and any tax treatment stated plainly
- **Payment terms and a due date**: "Net 14 — due [specific date]," not "due on receipt" vagueness
- Payment method details that make paying effortless: bank transfer details or payment link
- Your late-payment term (next section), printed calmly on the invoice itself

Send the invoice with the deliverable it pays for, the same day. An invoice sent three weeks late teaches the client that your money is not urgent.

## Payment terms that protect you

Agree these in the proposal/contract (Lesson 05), before work exists:

- **Terms length:** Net 7–14 is normal for a solo professional; Net 30 appears with larger companies — price and plan for it (Lesson 03) rather than resenting it.
- **Late fee / interest:** a stated, lawful late-payment charge (for example a fixed fee or monthly percentage, as your jurisdiction allows). Its main value is not collection — it is signaling that your terms are real.
- **Pause clause:** if an interim invoice goes unpaid past its due date, remaining work pauses until it is settled. Written in advance, this is professional; improvised mid-fight, it is a crisis.
- **Report release:** many testers release the full detailed report on balance settlement, giving an executive summary at delivery. If you use this structure, it must be in the signed agreement from day one — never invented at delivery time.

## When payment is late (it will happen)

Stay calm, factual, and in writing. Most late payment is disorganization, not malice — in a company, *your* invoice is competing with a process. A professional escalation ladder:

1. **Day 1 past due — friendly nudge.** "Invoice #12 for [engagement] was due on [date] — resending it here in case it got buried. Let me know if your finance team needs anything from me."
2. **Day 7–10 — firmer, still polite.** Restate amount, original terms, and the late term from the agreement. Ask for a specific payment date, and get it in writing.
3. **Day 21–30 — consequences, stated plainly.** "Under our agreement, late charges apply from [date], and further work (including the pending retest) is paused until this is settled. I'd like to resolve this this week — please confirm the payment date."
4. **Beyond that —** stop all work, keep records perfect, and escalate through formal channels available in your jurisdiction (demand letter, small-claims, or a lawyer's letter) if the amount justifies it. What you never do: threaten, shame publicly, or — worst of all — hint at using knowledge of their vulnerabilities as leverage. That is extortion, it is a crime, and it ends careers. Your only leverage is process and reputation, exercised calmly.

Throughout: one thread, dates and amounts exact, no emotion. The tester who handles a late payment with grace often keeps the client; the one who explodes loses the money *and* the reference.

## Checklist

- [ ] Deposit collected before any testing window is reserved
- [ ] Every invoice: complete details, exact amount, specific due date, effortless payment method
- [ ] Terms (length, late charge, pause clause) live in the signed agreement, not in my hopes
- [ ] Invoices sent the same day as the deliverable they pay for
- [ ] Late-payment ladder written and followed calmly, in writing, without threats — ever
