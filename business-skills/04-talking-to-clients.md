# Lesson 04 — Talking to Clients

*Unlocked by completing Category 04 at 100%. Business Skills ladder, step 4 of 11.*

Clients decide whether to trust you long before they see your technical work — usually in the first conversation. Talking to clients is not "sales tricks." It is structured curiosity: understanding their situation well enough to know whether you can help, and communicating like someone they can safely hand production access to.

## The discovery call

The discovery call is a 20–30 minute conversation before any proposal (Lesson 05). Its purpose is not to pitch. Its purpose is to diagnose. A simple structure:

1. **Their world first (5 min).** "Tell me about the product — who uses it, what does it do?" Let them talk. You are listening for what the application *is* in business terms: payments? health data? B2B accounts?
2. **The trigger (5 min).** "What made you look for a test now?" There is always a trigger: a customer demanding it, a funding round, a scare, a compliance deadline. The trigger tells you the real deadline and the real buyer pressure.
3. **The target (10 min).** Walk toward scope: which apps, which roles, any API, staging or production, have they been tested before, who fixes findings. You are informally building Lesson 02's document.
4. **Constraints (5 min).** Timeline, budget range (ask directly — professionals do), decision-maker ("who else needs to approve this?"), and their fixing capacity. A client with no developer time cannot use a 40-finding report.
5. **Next step (2 min).** Never end with "I'll send something." End with: "I'll send a written proposal with scope and price by Thursday. If it looks right, we sign the authorization and book the kickoff." A date, a document, a decision path.

## Questions that win trust

- "What would a bad outcome look like for you — what data or function worries you most?" *(Shows you think in their risk, not your payloads.)*
- "Has the app been tested before? What happened with the findings?" *(Reveals whether they fix things or shelve reports.)*
- "Who will receive the report, and who fixes issues?" *(You are planning their success, not just your test.)*
- "Is there anything in the app you're already suspicious about?" *(Founders usually know. And when you test exactly that area well, trust is earned.)*
- "What does your timeline depend on?" *(A launch date or customer deadline changes everything about scheduling.)*

Notice: none of these show off. Trust comes from asking about *their* problem precisely, then speaking plainly. Speak about impact in business language — "an attacker could see other customers' invoices" — never in vulnerability-name soup.

## First messages and outreach

Cold outreach works when it is specific, brief, and honest. It fails when it is fear-mongering ("your site is vulnerable!!") or fake familiarity. Two honest templates:

**Warm/referral or engaged-prospect message:**

> Hi [Name] — I help [type of company] find and fix web application vulnerabilities before attackers do, through manual penetration testing. If a security review of [their product] is on your roadmap this quarter, I'd be glad to send a short scope and fixed price — no obligation. Either way, happy to share a sample report so you can judge the quality first.

**Reply to an inbound enquiry:**

> Thanks for reaching out. Before I quote anything, I'd like to understand the application properly — the scope decides the price, and a wrong guess helps neither of us. Do you have 20 minutes this week for a short call? I'll ask about the app, the timeline, and what prompted the test, then send a written proposal with exact scope and price.

Rules for all outreach: never claim to have found vulnerabilities you have not found; never test first and ask later (that is a crime, not marketing — Lesson 05); never spam hundreds of identical messages; follow up once, politely, then stop.

## Red-flag clients — walk away early

- **"Just quickly hack it and tell us if it's secure."** No scope, no rigor, guaranteed dispute. Decline or educate once — then decline.
- **No one can sign the authorization.** If the person you're talking to cannot prove authority over the target, there is no engagement (Lesson 05).
- **"Test this site — it's my competitor's / my employer's, I just want to check."** Absolutely not. Ever.
- **Pressure to start before paperwork, payment terms, or scope.** Urgency that forbids process is how testers get burned.
- **They want you to hide or soften findings.** Your report is your integrity. A client who negotiates the truth is a liability.
- **They haggle the price down while expanding the scope.** Lesson 03: scope and price move together, on paper, or not at all.

Walking away politely is a business skill. Your reputation is worth more than any single engagement.

## Checklist

- [ ] My discovery calls follow a structure: their world → trigger → target → constraints → next step with a date
- [ ] I ask about their risk, fixing capacity, and decision-maker — not just the technology
- [ ] My outreach is specific and honest; I never claim findings I don't have
- [ ] I can recognize the six red flags and decline without drama
