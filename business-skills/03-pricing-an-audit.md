# Lesson 03 — Pricing an Audit

*Unlocked by completing Category 03 at 100%. Business Skills ladder, step 3 of 11.*

Pricing is where talented testers quietly go broke. They charge for the hours they *hope* the test takes, forget the report, forget the retest, forget the sales calls — and discover their real hourly rate is embarrassing. This lesson gives you honest models and the logic behind them. All figures below are **illustrative examples** to teach the framework, not market data and not any real company's prices. Your market, costs, and skill level set your numbers.

## The three pricing models

**1. Fixed price per engagement.** One number for a defined scope. Clients love the certainty; you carry the estimation risk. Works well once you can estimate reliably — which is why beginners should scope tightly (Lesson 02) and price small, well-defined apps first.

**2. Day rate.** You sell days of testing; the scope flexes within the purchased days. Simpler to estimate, common with agencies and repeat clients. Your day rate is your anchor number — everything else derives from it.

**3. Tiered packages.** Pre-defined levels (for example: a focused assessment of one small application; a standard test of a typical SaaS app; a deep test of a large platform with multiple roles and APIs). Tiers make buying easy and frame the conversation around depth, not hours. Most solo testers end up here.

Whatever the model, the price is built the same way underneath: **days of work × your day rate**, where "work" includes testing, reporting, meetings, and the retest.

## What actually drives the price

- **Application size** — number of distinct features, pages, endpoints, and user roles. A five-page marketing site with a login and a 200-endpoint platform are different universes.
- **Roles and accounts** — every privilege level multiplies authorization testing. Two roles is not twice one role; it is a matrix.
- **Test depth** — scanner-assisted check vs. full manual business-logic testing are different products. Say which one you are selling.
- **APIs and integrations** — a documented API adds surface; an undocumented one adds discovery time.
- **Reporting and retest** — a real report commonly takes 20–30% of total engagement effort. A retest cycle is more days. If they are not in your price, they are unpaid work.
- **Timeline pressure** — a rush job that displaces other work costs more. That is normal; say it plainly.

## Worked example (illustrative)

Assume an example day rate of **$400** — chosen for easy math, not as a recommendation.

| Example engagement | Testing | Report | Retest | Total days | Example price |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Small web app, 1–2 roles, standard depth | 3 days | 1 day | 0.5 day | 4.5 | ~$1,800 |
| Typical SaaS app, 3 roles + API | 5 days | 1.5 days | 1 day | 7.5 | ~$3,000 |
| Large platform, deep manual testing | 10 days | 2.5 days | 1.5 days | 14 | ~$5,600 |

Change the day rate and the table rescales. The lesson is the structure: price the *whole engagement*, not just the fun part with the terminal open.

## What never to do

- **Never race to the bottom.** There will always be someone cheaper. Competing on "cheapest" attracts the worst clients and makes the work unsustainable. Compete on clarity, evidence, and professionalism.
- **Never price before scope.** A number given before Lesson 02's document exists is a guess you will regret. "I can give you an exact price once we've defined the scope — that protects us both" is a professional sentence, not an evasion.
- **Never hide the retest.** Either it is included (priced, defined) or it is a paid add-on (priced, defined). "Free unlimited retests" is a debt you cannot pay.
- **Never discount by shrinking silently.** If a client needs a lower price, reduce scope openly: fewer roles, shallower depth, smaller app. The document changes, the price changes. Quietly doing the same work for less trains everyone to undervalue it.
- **Never apologize for the price.** State it, state what it includes, stop talking. Confidence is part of what they are buying (Lesson 01).

## A sane way to set your first day rate

Estimate your target monthly income, divide by realistic billable days (not 22 — sales, admin, and learning eat half a freelancer's time; 10–12 billable days is honest for many solo operators), and sanity-check against what your market pays testers of your proven level. Raise it as your proof grows (Lesson 11). Your labs — 110 completed applications with a certificate trail — are exactly the kind of proof that supports a serious rate.

## Checklist

- [ ] I have a day rate, derived from my costs and target income — not copied or guessed
- [ ] Every quote I send accounts for testing + report + meetings + retest
- [ ] I never quote before scope is written
- [ ] If price must drop, scope drops on paper — never silently
- [ ] My tiers (if I use them) differ by depth and size, clearly described
